Sahayta demo video — voiceover only (Variant A, "One Photo", 2:30)
TTS-ready. Read at a calm, unhurried pace. Pronunciation hints are in
parentheses — do NOT read the parentheses aloud, they are for the speaker.
"2G" = "two gee". Numbers are written out.

[1] Every monsoon, floods hit India. Phones work, networks don't, and help
can't find the people who need it. This is Sahayta — a disaster-response
O S for India.

[2] A citizen in Patna — Kankarbagh, pronounced KUN-ker-bagh — photographs
rising water. No English needed. No account. No manual. A photo, her own
words, a pin on the map — sixty seconds, even on a shaky two-gee connection.

[3] Sahayta's A I reads the photo and the Hindi text: severity 4 out of 5,
rescue category, critical priority — in seconds. And notice the label:
rule-fallback-v-one. It works with zero A P I keys, because in a disaster
you can't depend on one.

[4] Matching is skills, times distance, times availability. The nearest
qualified volunteer: Ravi Kumar, 2.1 kilometers away, match score 92.5.
He accepts. He's en route. And everyone watching the board sees it happen
live.

[5] One message, typed once, goes out in ten Indian languages — Hindi,
Hinglish, Bengali, Tamil, Telugu, Marathi, Gujarati, Kannada, Malayalam,
Punjabi — each short enough for S M S, because S M S is what reaches people
when data doesn't.

[6] The district official sees everything on one screen: the live S O S map,
a risk score of 82 with the reasons behind it, who's available, and a
minute-by-minute incident timeline. Even the simulated weather feed is
labeled — because in a disaster, trust is the feature.

[7] Networks die in disasters — so reports queue offline and sync without
duplicates. And the early-warning engine was already watching the sky.

[8] One photo. One volunteer. One district that knew what was coming.
That's Sahayta.
